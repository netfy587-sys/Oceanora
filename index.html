What This Guide Is All About

This website explains the ocean and the animals and plants that live in it, in simple language. It is written for students, travellers, families and anyone curious about the sea, with no science background needed.

Why the ocean matters

The ocean covers about 71 percent of Earth’s surface and holds most of the planet’s water. It moves heat around the globe, shapes weather and absorbs a large amount of the carbon dioxide we release. Tiny floating plants called phytoplankton produce a large share of the oxygen in our air, and the sea provides food and work for billions of people.

The ocean in layers

Scientists divide the open ocean by depth, because light, pressure and temperature change as you go down. The sunlit layer reaches about 200 metres and holds most sea life. Below it, the twilight layer runs to about 1,000 metres, where light fades to a dim blue. Deeper still is the dark midnight layer, followed by the abyss and the deep trenches below roughly 6,000 metres.

Pressure rises quickly with depth, and the water becomes cold and still. Even so, animals live at every level. Many deep-sea species make their own light, called bioluminescence, to find food, attract partners or confuse enemies.

Who lives in the sea

Marine life begins with plankton, the tiny drifting organisms that feed almost everything else. Above them are fish, from small schooling species to large sharks, as well as mammals such as whales and dolphins, and invertebrates such as jellyfish, crabs, squid and octopus. Sea turtles, seabirds and seals also depend on the ocean.

Coral reefs cover only a small fraction of the seafloor, yet they shelter roughly a quarter of all known marine species. That is why scientists treat reefs as some of the most valuable habitats on Earth.

Challenges the ocean faces

Plastic pollution, overfishing, warming water and rising acidity all put pressure on marine life. Warm water can cause corals to lose their colour and food, a process called bleaching. Waste and fishing nets can trap or harm animals of every size.

What you can do

Small habits help. Use fewer single-use plastics, choose seafood from sustainable sources, join a local beach clean and learn about the water near you. Reading about the ocean is a good first step, because people protect what they understand.

Quick Facts

  • Five named oceans: Pacific, Atlantic, Indian, Southern and Arctic
  • Average ocean depth is about 3.7 kilometres
  • The deepest known point, Challenger Deep, is almost 11 kilometres down
  • Sea water is about 3.5 percent salt on average
  • Much of the deep sea has never been seen directly

Questions People Ask

Short answers to common questions, so you can understand the basics quickly.

How many oceans are there?

Five are usually named: the Pacific, Atlantic, Indian, Southern and Arctic. They are all connected and form one global ocean.

Where is the deepest part of the ocean?

The deepest known spot is Challenger Deep in the Mariana Trench in the western Pacific, almost 11 kilometres below the surface.

Why is sea water salty?

Rain and rivers wash small amounts of minerals from rocks into the sea. Water evaporates, but the salts stay behind, so the sea slowly becomes salty.

Is the deep sea really unexplored?

A great deal of it has never been seen directly. Scientists map the seafloor with sonar and visit deep places with robots and special submarines, but much remains to be discovered.
